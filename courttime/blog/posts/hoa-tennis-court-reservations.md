Community courts are one of the most-used amenities an HOA has. They are also one of the most complained about. Court booking usually lands on a volunteer board member's plate with no real system behind it: a clipboard at the gate, a group text that's been running since 2018, or a clunky booking system the community signed up for years ago and nobody likes using.

This guide walks through what a good setup looks like for an HOA, and the decisions your board should make before choosing any tool.

## The problems most HOAs are actually solving

Before talking about software, it helps to name the real problems. For most communities, the list looks like this:

- **Non-residents using the courts.** Friends of friends, former residents, people from the next neighborhood over.
- **A few households dominating prime time.** Evening and weekend slots get claimed by the same people every week.
- **No-shows.** Courts sit empty while someone else wanted to play.
- **Pickleball versus tennis.** Shared or converted courts create scheduling tension between the two groups.
- **Volunteer burnout.** Whoever manages bookings becomes the person everyone texts.

A good reservation system should fix each of these, not just move the clipboard online or bolt booking onto software built for something else.

## 1. Keep the courts for residents

The most important HOA-specific feature is **verifying residency**. The cleanest approach is an **address whitelist**. The board uploads the list of addresses in the community, usually the same resident list the management company already keeps. Only people at those addresses can join.

Pair that with a **limit on accounts per address**. A household gets its members, but one address can't hand out logins to a dozen friends.

When someone moves out, removing the address cuts off access. Nobody has to have an awkward conversation.

## 2. Think in households

HOA membership comes with the home, so your booking rules should too. Set limits **per household** as well as per person:

- Maximum active reservations per household
- A cap on peak-hour bookings per household
- Per-person limits that still apply inside a household

This stops a family of five from holding five prime-time courts at once. Kids and spouses still get their own accounts and their own booking history.

## 3. Set a short, fair booking window

For a community with only two to four courts, a **booking window of 3 to 7 days** usually works best. Add a **release time**, such as courts opening at 7 a.m. seven days out, so the early-to-bed crowd isn't at a disadvantage.

Weekend mornings and weekday evenings deserve their own rules. A **peak-hours cap**, for example one prime-time booking per household per week, will do more for fairness than anything else you set.

We cover these rules in more depth in [How to Set Fair Court Booking Rules](/blog/fair-court-booking-rules/).

## 4. Handle tennis and pickleball on the same courts

Many HOAs have added pickleball lines to tennis courts, or converted one court entirely. That works fine, as long as the system knows what each court is:

- Label each court as **tennis, pickleball, or dual purpose** so players can filter for what they want
- Allow a **different maximum duration** for tennis and pickleball if your community wants shorter pickleball sessions
- Split a court into multiple pickleball courts in the system when it is lined for more than one game

## 5. Make no-shows and waivers someone else's problem

Two things boards hate doing by hand: chasing no-shows and collecting waivers.

- A **strike system** gives automatic strikes for no-shows and late cancellations, with a short booking lockout after too many. Members police themselves once they know it's there.
- **Online waiver and rules acceptance** at sign-up means every resident has agreed to the same court rules. The board doesn't need a filing cabinet of signed forms.

## 6. Communicate in the same place people book

Court closures, resurfacing, and new rules all need to reach residents. Look for **announcements and email updates** built into the booking tool. Then you're not juggling a separate mailing list that's always out of date.

A **community bulletin board** helps too: players looking for a fourth for doubles, ladder signups, social events. These are the things that currently clog up the neighborhood group text.

## What it should cost

Court booking for an HOA should not be a major budget line. Many tools are priced for large commercial clubs, with per-member fees, setup fees, and payment processing markups.

For a typical community with a few courts, look for **flat annual pricing based on court count** with no per-member charges. CourtTime, for example, is $50 per court per year, with a $200 minimum and a $550 maximum, and members always join for free.

## A simple rollout plan

1. **Board decides the rules.** Use the defaults above as a starting point and agree on them before launch.
2. **Upload the address list** and set the per-address account limit.
3. **Set up courts** with type, lights, and hours.
4. **Announce it** with a short email, a sign at the court gate, and a QR code to sign up.
5. **Run it for 30 days**, then look at real usage and adjust peak hours and limits.

Most communities find that once booking is fair and automatic, the complaints simply stop. The board member who used to manage the clipboard gets their evenings back.
