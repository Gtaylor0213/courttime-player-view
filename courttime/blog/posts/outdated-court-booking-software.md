Plenty of clubs and HOAs signed up for a court booking system years ago and never looked back. It works, mostly. Members grumble, staff have their workarounds, and switching feels like more trouble than it's worth.

But court booking software has changed a lot. What felt modern ten years ago now quietly costs you volunteer hours, frustrated members, and money. Here is how to tell whether your system has fallen behind.

## 1. It was built for a desktop, not a phone

Most members book from their phone, often standing at the court gate. Older systems were designed for a desktop browser. On a phone that means pinching and zooming, tiny buttons, and logins that time out halfway through a booking.

When booking is painful, people stop bothering and just show up. That leads to exactly the conflicts the system was supposed to prevent.

**What modern looks like:** a booking flow built for phones first, and a real app on the App Store and Google Play.

## 2. Your rules live in people's heads

"Max two bookings a week" and "no booking more than a week ahead" only work if the software actually enforces them. Many older systems offer a single booking window and little else, so the real rules end up enforced by a board member sending awkward emails.

**What modern looks like:** rules that enforce themselves at booking time, including peak-hour caps, weekend policies, release times, and maximum active reservations. Members get a clear message explaining why a booking isn't allowed. For more on this, see [How to Set Fair Court Booking Rules](/blog/fair-court-booking-rules/).

## 3. No-shows have no consequences

If your system can't tell who booked a court and never showed up, habitual no-shows never face any consequence. Courts sit empty while other members are turned away.

**What modern looks like:** no-shows and late cancellations are tracked, with an optional strike system that briefly locks out repeat offenders.

## 4. It doesn't understand households

Families, couples, and HOA residences share a membership. Older software often treats every account as an independent individual, so a family of four can hold four times as many prime-time courts as everyone else.

**What modern looks like:** per-household limits, a cap on accounts per address, and family accounts that still keep each person's own booking history.

## 5. Anyone can get in

For HOAs and private clubs, keeping courts for members is the whole point. If your system relies on a shared password or an approval queue nobody checks, non-members will find their way onto the courts.

**What modern looks like:** member approval, and for HOAs an address whitelist so only residents can sign up.

## 6. Communication happens somewhere else

Court closures, rain-outs, and rule changes go out through a separate email list or a group text, and half the members miss them.

**What modern looks like:** announcements, email updates, and a community bulletin board in the same app members already use to book.

## 7. You're paying for features you don't use

Many court systems are priced for large commercial facilities, with per-member fees, setup charges, and payment processing markups. A small club or HOA ends up paying enterprise prices for a calendar.

**What modern looks like:** simple pricing based on the number of courts, with no per-member fees.

## Old system vs. modern booking app

| | Outdated booking software | Modern booking app |
|---|---|---|
| **Phone experience** | Desktop site squeezed onto a phone | Built for phones, with a native app |
| **Rules** | A booking window and not much else | Peak hours, weekends, release times, and limits, all enforced automatically |
| **No-shows** | Invisible | Tracked, with optional strikes and lockouts |
| **Households** | Every account on its own | Per-household and per-address limits |
| **Who can book** | Shared logins or manual approval | Member approval and address verification |
| **Announcements** | Separate email list | Built in |
| **Pricing** | Per-member fees and add-ons | Flat, based on court count |

## How to switch without a revolt

Change is the hard part, not the software. A few things make it painless:

1. **Keep the rules you already have** for the first month. Changing the system and the rules at the same time is too much at once.
2. **Make signing up quick.** Share one sign-up link, and for an HOA, upload your resident address list so residents are recognized as soon as they join.
3. **Pick a switchover date** and announce it a couple of weeks ahead.
4. **Put a QR code on the court gate** that links to sign-up.
5. **Share early wins**: fewer conflicts, open slots that are easy to find, and no more group-text chaos.

## The cost question

It's worth comparing on **total annual cost**, including per-member fees, add-on modules, and payment processing, not just the advertised base price. Many small clubs and HOAs find that a flat, court-based price costs less than the system they have now.
